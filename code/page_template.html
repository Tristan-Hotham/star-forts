    In the 1490s Italian engineers worked out that a round tower could not stand up to cannon, and that a wall could only be defended if every foot of it could be shot along from another part of the wall. The answer was the bastion: a low, angled platform of earth faced with brick or stone, pushed out from the corners so that the guns on each one swept the faces of its neighbours. Seen from above, a ring of bastions makes a star. Within a few decades the star fort was being built in Antwerp and Berwick, and soon after on the coasts of India, Africa, Brazil and the Caribbean. This map follows it round the world.

    Every symbol above is one of {{counts.main|int}} bastioned forts, citadels and fortress towns. {{counts.outlines|int}} of them ({{counts.outline_pct|pc}}) still have a star-shaped outline traced in OpenStreetMap, and from zoom 11 the map draws each one along that outline, with its moat outside and its rampart inside. The rest are known only as a point in Wikidata or, for {{counts.hand_added|int}} famous forts with no traced outline, a point I added from English Wikipedia. Slide the timeline to see the forts with a date appear decade by decade, the newest in red ink. Tap any fort for its date, where that date comes from, its size and links to its photographs.

    01 · The beltWhere the stars are

    The forts form a belt across Europe rather than a scatter. {{counts.europe_pct|pc}} of them are in Europe, and the thickest part of the belt runs along the old war zones: the Low Countries, northern France and the Rhine, the Alps, the Venetian and Habsburg frontiers with the Ottomans, and the Baltic coasts fought over by Sweden, Denmark and Russia. The densest one-degree square on the map is around the Rhine and Maas delta in the {{densest_cell.country|raw}}, with {{densest_cell.n|int}} forts. Per square kilometre, Malta has the most by far ({{density.0.n|int}} forts on an island group smaller than the Isle of Wight), then the Netherlands ({{density.1.per_10k_km2|f1}} per 10,000 km²) and Belgium.

    Two countries look larger than life because of who has catalogued what. Sweden has {{sweden.n|int}} forts here, almost all of them ({{sweden.catalogue|int}}) small skansar (redoubts and sconces) recorded in Wikidata from the Swedish heritage register, with no traced outline. A further {{counts.bavarian|int}} entries from the Bavarian monument list, also labelled sconces in Wikidata, are of uncertain form and age, so I keep them off the main map and out of the counts; you can switch them on in the Layers panel. Beyond Europe the forts are fewer ({{counts.beyond_europe|int}}) but far better known: {{regions.10.dated_pct|pc}} of those in Asia and Oceania and {{regions.9.dated_pct|pc}} of those in the Americas have a date, against {{regions.5.dated_pct|pc}} in Scandinavia.

    02 · The spreadFrom Italy, by land and by sea

    Only {{dates.dated|int}} of the {{counts.main|int}} forts ({{dates.dated_pct|pc}}) have a date I can trust for their bastions. {{dates.nonhand|int}} come from Wikidata or from the start date in OpenStreetMap; I added {{dates.hand|int}} more by hand from English Wikipedia for the forts that tell the story, so the dated set leans towards the famous. With that caveat, the shape is clear. The earliest dated works are Italian, from {{regions.0.first_name|raw}} ({{regions.0.first_year|raw}}) to the Fortezza da Basso in Florence (1534). By the 1540s and 1550s bastions were going up at Jülich, Fort Rammekens on the Scheldt, Berwick and Landskrona, and in the same decades at Diu in India, Mazagan in Morocco and San Juan in Puerto Rico.

    That is the striking thing: the bastion did not creep outwards from Italy. The distance from Florence to a country's first dated fort hardly predicts its date (a rank correlation of {{diffusion.rho|raw}} across {{diffusion.n|int}} countries). The median first date for the European countries in the data is {{diffusion.median_first_europe|raw}}, and for the countries beyond Europe {{diffusion.median_first_beyond|raw}}. Portuguese and Spanish ships carried the idea to Asia, Africa and the Americas as early as overland routes carried it to the north of Europe.

    03 · The colonial chainPortugal first, Japan last

    Of the forts I dated by hand, {{colonial_n|int}} lie outside Europe. Their order follows the empires. Portugal came first, fortifying its trading posts on the Indian Ocean: Diu (1535), Mazagan (1541), Mozambique Island (1558), Daman (1559) and Fort Jesus at Mombasa (1593), designed by the Milanese engineer Giovanni Battista Cairati. Spain fortified the gates of its treasure fleets: El Morro at San Juan (1539), Havana (1589) and Manila (1590). The Dutch East India Company followed in the 1600s, from Fort Belgica on the Banda Islands (1611) and Fort Zeelandia on Taiwan (1624) to the pentagonal Castle of Good Hope at Cape Town (1666). The English East India Company built Fort St George at Madras in 1640; Denmark built Christiansvaern on St Croix in 1738; France built Louisbourg on Cape Breton Island from 1720.

    The last link is the strangest. In 1857 the Tokugawa shogunate began Goryōkaku at Hakodate, a five-pointed star laid out on the French model, three and a half centuries after the first Italian bastions and just as rifled artillery was making them obsolete.

    04 · The great fortress townsStars you can walk round

    Most star forts are small. Inside the {{sizes.n|int}} traced outlines the median area is {{sizes.median_ha|f1}} hectares, about {{sizes.median_diameter_m|int}} m across; half are under 2 ha, and {{sizes.points_4_6_pct|pc}} have four, five or six points, the square, pentagon and hexagon of the textbooks. A few are whole towns. Palmanova, the nine-pointed ideal city Venice founded in 1593, encloses {{famous.0.area_ha|f1}} ha inside its outline as mapped; Naarden, rebuilt from 1673 with a double ring of bastions and moats, {{famous.1.area_ha|f1}} ha; Elvas, the largest dry-ditch bastioned fortification in the world, {{famous.5.area_ha|f1}} ha with {{famous.5.points|int}} points. Only {{sizes.over_10ha|int}} outlines enclose more than 10 ha.

    05 · SurvivorsWhat is left on the ground

    Star forts were built to be hard to remove, and many outlived their purpose as parks, moats and street plans. {{survival.outline_pct|pc}} of the forts on the map have an outline in OpenStreetMap that my shape test can still recognise as a star; beyond Europe the share is {{survival.beyond_outline_pct|pc}}, in Europe {{survival.europe_outline_pct|pc}}. That is not a census of survival: a missing outline can mean a fort that was levelled, one that survives but is mapped as loose pieces, or one too small or eroded to pass the test. Of the {{survival.hand_n|int}} forts I dated by hand, {{survival.hand_with_outline_pct|pc}} have a traced outline, and {{survival.hand_vanished|int}} are known to have been largely demolished: the citadels of Antwerp and Turin, Saarlouis, Fort Amsterdam on Manhattan and Louisbourg (now partly rebuilt).

    06 · How I did thisShapes, dates and doubts

    The forts. The outlines come from The Inked World, which searched OpenStreetMap for every fort, fortification, town wall and moat and kept the closed shapes that look like a bastioned trace: at least four sharp salient corners that stand out from the centre at similar distances, with re-entrant corners between them, filling less than 93% of their convex hull. To these it added Wikidata's star forts, sconces and polygonal forts that have no traced outline. I matched each outline to the OpenStreetMap features lying on it for names and start dates, placed Wikidata's fortified towns on the outlines that contain them, and gave the {{counts.unnamed_outlines|int}} that still had no name the name of the nearest town in Natural Earth.

    Look-alikes. The shape test also finds the towered walls of Chinese and Korean towns, Mughal and Rajput forts, and the walls of North African medinas, whose towers can make a star-like outline. I keep the {{counts.lookalike|int}} outlines in those countries out of the counts unless I dated them by hand as a European work, and you can switch them on to judge for yourself.

    Dates. For each fort I took, in order: my hand date from English Wikipedia; Wikidata's start time or opening date; Wikidata's inception if precise to the year or decade; then OpenStreetMap's start date. Dates before 1450 are the medieval origin of a site, not its bastions, and are set aside ({{dates.medieval_only|int}} forts). An inception can still be the date a site was first fortified rather than when it was bastioned, so treat single dates with care.

    What this cannot say. The map shows what has been mapped and catalogued, which is far from everything that was built: thousands of earthwork sconces and siege works have gone, and catalogues are richer in some countries than others. Most forts have no date, and the dated ones are the famous ones. The size of a fort is the area inside the line the shape test found, which is sometimes the inner rampart and sometimes the outer moat.
